How to choose wine from a restaurant wine list

Reviewed 17 November 2026 · By the PocSomm team · Sources below

Set a budget, decide glass or bottle, and pick a style that suits the food. Then choose the best-value bottle in that range, and let the sommelier help. In the US, if your table will drink three or more glasses of one wine, the bottle is usually cheaper [1][2]. Don't avoid the second-cheapest bottle: the evidence says it's marked up less than the ones above it, not more [3].

1. Set a budget first

Decide what you want to spend before you read the list. A long list is much easier once you only look at one price range. It also protects you from the pull of the bottle everyone has heard of, which is rarely the best value on the page.

2. Glass or bottle?

In the US, a single 5-ounce glass is commonly priced at what the restaurant paid for the whole bottle [1], while the bottle itself is listed at about three times that cost [2]. So three glasses cost about as much as the bottle, and a 750 ml bottle pours five. If two or more of you want the same wine for the meal, order the bottle.

In the UK the gap is small: a London study found a 175 ml glass cost on average only 13.1% more per millilitre than the bottle [3]. There, ordering by the glass is a good way to try different wines without paying much extra.

3. Match the style to the food

You don't need to know every grape. A few classic rules of thumb cover most dinners:

  • Match weight with weight. Delicate fish with a light white; a braise or a steak with a fuller red.
  • Acidity loves rich and acidic food. Crisp whites and high-acid reds cut through cream, butter and fried food, and stand up to tomato sauces.
  • Tannic reds want protein and fat. Red meat softens the grip of a young Cabernet or Nebbiolo.
  • Spicy food: off-dry, lower-alcohol whites such as Riesling calm the heat; high alcohol makes it burn more.
  • Dessert: the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert, or it tastes sour.

Ordering one bottle for different dishes? Pick something versatile with good acidity and moderate weight: sparkling wine, dry Riesling, Pinot Noir and Gamay (Beaujolais) go with a wide range of food.

4. Find the value in your range

Compare each candidate's list price with what it costs in a shop. About 2 times is normal in the US, about 3 times in the UK [2][4]; 1.75 times or less is great value. The full method is in how to tell if a wine on a list is good value, and the markup calculator does the math.

  • Expensive bottles usually carry a lower multiple than cheap ones [2][5], so moving up a price step often means paying less markup for what's in the glass.
  • The second-cheapest bottle isn't a trap. In a study of 6,335 wines on London lists, it was marked up significantly less than the four bottles above it, and markups peaked mid-list [3].
  • Look for older vintages. Restaurants sometimes price old stock below today's shop price: 38% of the UK lists in one analysis had at least one bottle under retail [4].

5. Use the sommelier

A good sommelier knows the list better than any guide. Give them three things:

  • What you're eating.
  • What you like, ideally a wine you've enjoyed ("we loved a Sancerre last week").
  • Your budget, without saying it: point at a bottle in your price range and say "something like this". Sommeliers read that as your budget.

No sommelier, or a list in a language you don't read? That's the moment for a photo and an app.

6. When the bottle arrives

  • Check the label: same producer, same wine, same vintage as the list. If the vintage differs, you can ask for the price to be checked or choose again.
  • The taste is for faults, not preferences. A musty, wet-cardboard smell suggests cork taint; send it back. If the wine is sound but not what you hoped for, it's polite to keep it.
  • Too warm? Ask for an ice bucket for a few minutes, even for a red.

Frequently asked questions

Should I order wine by the glass or by the bottle?

In the US, three glasses of one wine usually cost about as much as the bottle [1][2], so if your table will drink three or more glasses of it, order the bottle. In the UK, glasses cost only a little more per millilitre [3].

Is it bad to order the second-cheapest wine?

No. In a study of 6,335 wines on London lists it was marked up less than the four bottles above it [3].

How do I tell the sommelier my budget without saying it out loud?

Point at a bottle on the list in your price range and say you're thinking of something like this.

What should I do when the waiter pours a taste?

Check it isn't faulty. A musty, wet-cardboard smell suggests cork taint. If the wine is sound, accept it even if it isn't quite what you expected.
