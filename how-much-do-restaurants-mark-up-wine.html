How much do restaurants mark up wine?

Reviewed 17 November 2026 · By the PocSomm team · Every figure links to its source below

In the US, a bottle on a restaurant list usually costs about three times what the restaurant paid for it, which is roughly twice what the same bottle costs in a shop [1][2]. UK lists run higher against shop prices: about three times retail on average [8]. Wine by the glass usually carries the biggest markup, because a single glass is often priced at what the restaurant paid for the whole bottle [6].

Why "three times cost" means "twice the shop price"

Restaurants and shops buy from the same wholesalers. A US shop typically sells a bottle at about 1.5 times what it paid; a restaurant at about 3 times. In one winery's worked example, a bottle that costs a restaurant $16.67 sells for $25 in a shop and $50 on a list [2]. Wine Spectator puts it the same way: "about three times the wholesale price, or about twice the price of retail" [1].

That price covers more than the wine: storage, glassware, breakage, a sommelier's time, and service. Markups have crept up over the years. In 2002 Wine Spectator described 2.5 to 3 times wholesale as the standard [1]; in 2024 New York sommeliers described 3 to 3.5 times as the norm [3], and in 2026 US beverage directors described 2.5 to 4 times [4].

Wine by the glass

In the US, the common rule is that one 5-ounce glass is priced at the bottle's wholesale cost [6]. A 750 ml bottle pours about five such glasses, so the bottle earns about five times what it cost, which is more than the same wine would earn sold as a bottle. Wine Spectator: "The biggest markup, proportionally, is usually for wines by the glass" [1].

The UK is different. In a study of London restaurants, a 175 ml glass cost on average only 13.1% more per millilitre than the same wine bought by the bottle [7]. Ordering by the glass there costs little extra; in the US it often costs a lot.

Cheap bottles, expensive bottles, and the second-cheapest myth

Restaurants usually apply a lower multiple to expensive wine. One New York sommelier gave the example of a $25 wholesale bottle listed at $75 (3×) and a $150 bottle listed at $275 (about 1.8×) [3]. You still pay more money for the expensive bottle, but you lose less of it to markup.

You may have heard that the second-cheapest bottle is the worst value, because restaurants know people order it to avoid looking cheap. The data say otherwise. A study of 6,335 wines on London lists found that "the mark-up on the second cheapest wine is significantly below that on the four next most expensive wines", and that percentage markups peak around the middle of the list [7]. Its conclusion: "It is therefore an urban myth that the second-cheapest wine is especially exploitative."

The UK

A restaurant critic's analysis of 278 wine lists, published in December 2024, found an average of 3.03 times the retail price including service, roughly 2.7 times before service [8]. The range was wide: the best-value London lists charged about 1.8 to 2 times retail, the priciest about 4.5 times, and 14% of lists had at least one bottle at ten times retail. UK multiples look high partly because duty and VAT make up a large share of a UK bottle, so restaurants pay only slightly less than shops do: "the trade price is often only a couple of pounds less than the cheapest retail price" [9].

Sweden

Swedish restaurants mostly buy through licensed wholesalers and importers; the law also lets them buy from Systembolaget [10]. The only price rule is a floor: a drink may not be served below its purchase price plus a reasonable markup ("inköpspriset … jämte skäligt påslag") [10]. There is no ceiling. One Swedish columnist's example: 500 kronor on the list for a bottle that costs the restaurant "en dryg hundralapp", a bit over 100 kronor [11].

Comparing a list price with the Systembolaget price is the natural check in Sweden, but read the result with care. Systembolaget adds only a small margin on top of tax and the supplier's price, so its shelf price is much closer to what a restaurant pays than a US shop price is. A wine listed at 3 times its Systembolaget price is therefore marked up more than "3 times the shop price" means in the US. (That's our reading of how the prices are built, not a published figure.) Many restaurant wines aren't sold at Systembolaget at all.

Bringing your own wine isn't an option: Swedish law forbids drinking alcohol on licensed premises other than what the restaurant serves under its license [10].

Elsewhere in Europe

Measured data are scarce. In Italy and France, "three times the purchase price" is the rule of thumb you'll hear [12]. A German trade magazine called "purchase price times three" outdated and noted that premium wines usually get a lower factor [13]. Treat these as rules of thumb, not measurements.

Bringing your own (corkage)

Where it's allowed, bringing your own bottle and paying a corkage fee can beat the list. In the US, fees generally run from $25 to $100 a bottle, with a few top restaurants at $150 to $200 [14]. Call ahead, and don't bring a wine that's already on the restaurant's list [14].

How to judge a bottle on a list

Divide the list price by what the same bottle (same producer, vintage and size) costs in a shop near you. Our calculator uses these bands:

  • 1.75× or less: great value. Clearly below the usual US markup of about twice the shop price, and better than even the best-value UK lists (about 1.8–2×).
  • 1.75× to 3×: fair price. The span from the typical US list to the UK average.
  • 3× or more: high markup. Above the UK average and above what US sommeliers call their ceiling.

Compare against a normal local shop price, not the cheapest price you can find online: the cheapest online price makes every markup look bigger [7]. And in the US, expect a glass to look expensive by this measure; that's how by-the-glass programs are priced [6].

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal markup on wine in a restaurant?

In the US, about three times what the restaurant paid the wholesaler, roughly twice the shop price. Sommeliers describe 3 to 3.5 times their cost as the norm and 4 times as a ceiling [3]. UK lists average about three times the retail price [8].

Is wine by the glass a bad deal?

In the US, usually yes: one 5-ounce glass is often priced at what the restaurant paid for the whole bottle [6]. In London, a 175 ml glass cost only about 13% more per millilitre than the bottle [7].

Is the second-cheapest wine on the list a rip-off?

No. In a study of 6,335 wines on London lists it was marked up significantly less than the next four bottles up; markups peak mid-list [7].

Are expensive wines marked up less?

As a multiple, usually yes: a $150 bottle may be listed at under twice its cost while a $25 bottle is listed at three times [3]. You still pay more in money terms.

Can I bring my own wine to a restaurant in Sweden?

No. Drinking alcohol on licensed premises other than what the restaurant serves is forbidden (Alkohollag 8 kap. 24 §) [10].
