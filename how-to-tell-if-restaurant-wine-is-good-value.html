How to tell if a wine on a restaurant list is good value

Reviewed 17 November 2026 · By the PocSomm team · Sources below

Divide the list price by what the same bottle costs in a shop. 1.75 times or less is great value, 1.75 to 3 times is a fair restaurant price, and 3 times or more is a high markup. For comparison, the usual US markup is about twice the shop price [1] and UK lists average about three times [2]. Expect cheap bottles and wine by the glass to come out higher, and expensive bottles lower.

Have both prices? The wine markup calculator does the division and works for glasses too.

Step 1: Pin down the exact wine

Value only makes sense for the same bottle. From the list, note:

  • Producer and wine name. "Chablis" alone could be a dozen producers at very different prices.
  • Vintage. Prices can differ a lot between years of the same wine. If the list gives no vintage, ask.
  • Bottle size. Half bottles and magnums aren't simply half or double the price of a standard bottle, so compare like with like.

When the bottle arrives, check that the label and vintage match what you ordered.

Step 2: Find the shop price

Use a normal shop price in the country you're in, including tax, not the cheapest price you can find anywhere online. A lower comparison price makes every markup look bigger. For example, a London study that compared lists with the lowest online price found a median of 3.7 times [3], while an analysis against average retail prices found about 3 times [2].

  • A local shop's website or a price-comparison site.
  • In Sweden and Norway, Systembolaget or Vinmonopolet. Their price is the natural benchmark, but it sits close to what restaurants themselves pay, so normal multiples there look higher (see step 4).
  • No time to look up every bottle? PocSomm reads the whole list from a photo and estimates each wine's local shop price. Those are estimates, so check the bottle you settle on if it matters.

Step 3: Divide

List price ÷ shop priceVerdictWhat it means
Below 1Below retailRare but real, usually an older vintage priced before its market rose [2][4]. Check the vintage.
Up to 1.75Great valueClearly below the usual US markup and better than even the best-value UK lists [1][2].
1.75 to 3Fair priceNormal restaurant pricing, from a typical US list to the UK average [1][2].
3 or moreHigh markupAbove what most restaurants charge. There's often a better-value bottle nearby.

Example: a bottle listed at 60 that costs 25 in a shop is 60 ÷ 25 = 2.4 times: a fair price. The same bottle at 40 would be 1.6 times: great value.

Step 4: Adjust for context

Cheap bottles carry the highest multiples

Restaurants usually apply a lower multiple to expensive wine [1][5]. A 2026 study of 1,403 bottles on 38 Japanese restaurant lists measured this directly: bottles under ¥3,000 retail sold for a median 3.92 times retail, ¥3,000 to ¥5,000 bottles for 2.75 times, and bottles over ¥50,000 for 1.35 times [4]. So a cheap bottle at 3 times may be ordinary for that list, while an expensive bottle at 3 times is steep.

That doesn't make the cheapest bottles a trap. In a large London study, the two cheapest wines on a list had lower percentage markups than the bottles just above them, and markups peaked mid-list [3]. The idea that the second-cheapest bottle is the worst deal didn't hold up.

Wine by the glass

In the US, one 5-ounce glass is commonly priced at what the restaurant paid for the whole bottle [6], so glasses usually come out as a high markup by this test. That's how by-the-glass programs are priced, not a sign of a bad restaurant. In London, glasses cost only about 13% more per millilitre than the bottle [3]. If two or more of you want the same wine, compare the bottle price with the glasses you'd order.

Where you are

  • US: about 2 times the shop price is normal [1].
  • UK: about 2.7 times before service and 3 times including it is average, partly because UK duty and VAT leave little gap between trade and shop prices [2][7].
  • Sweden and Norway: against the monopoly price, multiples run higher than against a US shop price, because the monopolies add only a small margin to what the supplier charges plus tax [8]. A Stockholm bottle at 3 times its Systembolaget price may still be an ordinary restaurant price. (That's our reading of how the prices are built, not a measured figure.)

Frequently asked questions

What counts as good value for wine in a restaurant?

1.75 times the shop price or less is great value; up to about 3 times is a normal restaurant price. The usual US markup is about twice the shop price [1]; UK lists average about three times [2].

Is the cheapest wine on the list the best value?

Not usually. Cheap bottles tend to carry the highest multiple of their shop price: in Japan, bottles under ¥3,000 retail sold for a median 3.92 times retail, against 1.35 times for bottles over ¥50,000 [4].

Can a restaurant wine cost less than in a shop?

Yes, occasionally, usually older vintages priced before their market value rose. In one analysis of 278 UK wine lists, 38% had at least one bottle below its retail price [2]; in the Japanese study, 8.7% of bottles were [4].
